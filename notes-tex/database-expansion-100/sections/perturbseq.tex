\section{Rows that bear on a yeast Perturb-seq\secstatus{todo}}\label{sec:perturbseq}

A Perturb-seq is defined by two dimensionalities, and a dataset earns a place here only by
being high on at least one of them.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \term{Input dimensionality} is the size of the perturbation space per cell:
    combinatorial edits, or a perturbation crossed with genetic background. A large library
    of single perturbations is not high-dimensional input. It is one edit per cell, sampled
    widely, and a model fit to it never sees two edits interact.
  \item \term{Output dimensionality} is the readout: a transcriptome or a per-cell
    distribution rather than a scalar fitness or titer.
\end{itemize}

That distinction removes a row this table previously carried. Momen-Roknabadi 2020 is a
genome-wide CRISPRi library, which is large, but it delivers one guide per cell and reads
out competitive fitness. It is low-dimensional on both axes and is no longer flagged; its
value is as a second library for testing guide-design transfer, not as Perturb-seq evidence.

\subsection{The quadrant that matters is nearly empty\secstatus{todo}}\label{sec:quadrant}

Of 156 candidates, 15 are high on the input axis, 15 on the output axis, and \textbf{three}
on both: Boocock 2025, Albert 2018 and N'Guessan 2025. All three reach the both-axes
quadrant the same way, by crossing recombinant natural genotypes with a transcriptome, and
none of them perturbs a gene deliberately.

So the gap is specific rather than general. Yeast has deep genotype-to-fitness data and deep
wild-type transcriptome data, and where the two meet it is through natural variation, not
through designed perturbation. The largest genotype-indexed transcriptome with designed
perturbations is Kemmeren 2014, already built, and it is 1,484 single deletions read out one
gene at a time, which is exactly the shallow case: one knockout, one profile, no interaction
measured. Sameith 2015, also built, adds double mutants but only 72 of them.

That is the argument for running the experiment, and it is also the specification. To land
in the empty quadrant a design needs a combinatorial perturbation per cell, not a larger
single-perturbation library, and a transcriptome readout rather than a growth score.

\subsection{Depth rather than breadth\secstatus{todo}}\label{sec:depth}

Three classes were added to this pass specifically because they go deep on one locus instead
of shallow across many, and every one of them carries an exactly known sequence per genotype.

\notesec{Deep mutational scans, 9 rows} Li 2016 and Puchta 2016 are near-complete fitness
landscapes of a single native yeast gene at roughly 65,000 and 60,000 variants. Domingo 2018
and Diss 2018 go further and measure pairwise mutations, so epistasis is resolved inside a
gene rather than between genes. Faure 2022 separates a variant's effect on folding from its
effect on binding by measuring both in the same library, which is what turns a landscape
mechanistic. These supply the input dimensionality the fitness screens lack.

\notesec{Regulatory DNA, 8 rows} de Boer 2020 is above 100 million exactly known promoter
sequences with a measured expression level each, which is the largest sequence-to-phenotype
dataset in yeast by orders of magnitude. Vaishnav 2022 extends it to a fitness landscape and
tests natural-isolate promoter variants against the random library, and Renganaath 2020
measures 5,832 real promoter alleles from natural variation. This is the answer to whether
promoter strength has been measured at scale: it has, both for random sequence and for
native alleles, and the two are on the same readout.

\notesec{Combinatorial genomes, 3 rows} SCRaMbLE genotypes carry tens of simultaneous
structural edits and are sequenced afterwards. A genotype there is a rearranged chromosome
rather than an allele, which is the deepest per-cell perturbation available and the hardest
thing a genotype-to-phenotype model will eventually be asked to predict on.

\subsection{The synergy question is answerable, and not yet answered\secstatus{todo}}

Whether one dataset predicts another decides how much new data is worth collecting, and the
list contains the pairs to test it before any is collected:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Same phenotype, different platform.} Cooper 2010 against the built
    Mulleder 2016, amino acids by capillary electrophoresis against mass spectrometry on an
    overlapping genotype axis.
  \item \textbf{Same phenotype, different method.} Liu 2021 against Kuroda 2019, isobutanol
    tolerance on the same collection by colony imaging against competitive fitness.
  \item \textbf{Same perturbation, different background.} Hale 2024, Galardini 2019 and
    She 2018, all of which vary the background while holding the perturbation fixed.
  \item \textbf{Different library, same modality.} McGlincy 2021 against
    Momen-Roknabadi 2020, two CRISPRi libraries with different vectors and design rules.
  \item \textbf{Cheap layer against expensive layer.} Caudal 2024 transcriptomes against
    Muenzner 2024 proteomes on overlapping isolates, and Skelly 2013, which measures both on
    the same 22 strains rather than joining across studies.
  \item \textbf{Synthetic sequence against natural allele.} de Boer 2020 and Vaishnav 2022
    against Renganaath 2020: whether a model trained on random promoters predicts the effect
    of a real one.
\end{itemize}

None of these transfers has been measured. Each is a hypothesis about what a model could
learn, and each becomes a run once both halves are ingested. The last pair is the cheapest
and the most immediately decisive.
