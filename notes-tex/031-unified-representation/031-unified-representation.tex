\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Five served datasets hold 7.48 million chemogenomic records, and training one model across
them requires a single input representation. They disagree on three axes. Ploidy runs from
haploid through homozygous diploid to heterozygous diploid. Compound panels run from 41 to
5{,}170. Dose runs from an unquantified target inhibition through a nine-order-of-magnitude
molar range to a single fixed concentration. Every number here is measured from the served
records through their loaders.

The genotype axis resolves cleanly. The schema already carries heterozygosity in two parts,
a genome-wide ploidy baseline and a per-locus copy-number deviation, and all five datasets
round-trip through it. A per-gene functional dose, copies present over copies in the
reference, is lossless for these five because every perturbation is either a full deletion
or a one-of-two variant, and it needs no separate ploidy feature because the vector's
background level is the ploidy. It is also learnable rather than merely expressible:
Hoepfner supplies 648{,}977 gene-by-compound cells measured both heterozygous and
homozygous. The gap is entirely in the tensor layer, which reads only a gene name and
therefore makes those 648{,}977 pairs byte-identical inputs carrying different responses.

The dose axis does not resolve. Forty of Vanacloig's 41 compounds carry no concentration at
all, only a target-inhibition basis; every Wildenhain compound sits at one fixed
concentration, so its dose column is constant; and for the 214 compounds dosed in more than
one dataset the median ratio between the highest and lowest dose is 12.5. Dose therefore
enters as a basis token rather than a number.

Chemically, pooling buys coverage of the phenolic inhibitors and not of the small alcohols,
and Wildenhain supplies essentially all of the gain. Yeast9 can share the molecule space
for the two thirds of its species that have a structure, and 103 of those are dosed
somewhere in the five datasets.
\end{abstract}
