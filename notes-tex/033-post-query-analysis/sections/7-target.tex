\notesec{What standardizing fixes}
Without it the loss is Wildenhain's. That source is 7.1 percent of the cells and 74.9
percent of the raw squared error, while Vanacloig, the application target, is 2.4 percent
of the cells and 0.2 percent of the squared error (Figure~\ref{fig:target}b). The plan
stated this qualitatively and the store gives it a size.

\notesec{What standardizing leaves}
Dividing by the standard deviation equalizes the second moment and nothing else, and the
four distributions are far from a common shape (Figure~\ref{fig:target}a). The excess
kurtosis runs from 15.5 to 61.2. The standard deviation is 1.6 to 6.7 times the robust
standard deviation, so which scale is divided out changes the relative scale of the
sources by up to a factor of four. And inside every source the squared error belongs to a small
minority: between 1.7 and 3.1 percent of cells lie beyond three standard deviations, and
they carry between 41 and 80 percent of their source's squared error
(Figure~\ref{fig:target}c). The sick tail reaches 7 to 10 standard deviations at the
0.1th percentile in all four sources, while the resistant tail reaches 5 in three of them
and 1 in Wildenhain.

\textit{Hypothesis (untested):} under a squared-error loss on this target the model is fit
mostly to the hits, and a per-compound correlation, which is how the evaluation scores,
weights every gene of a fold equally. Whether the mismatch costs anything is a first-round
question, and the measurement says where to look, not what will be found.

\notesec{Cross-source agreement is unchanged}
Table~\ref{tab:cross} repeats the plan's agreement test on the store's measurements for
the six pairs of the four kept sources. Five pairs reproduce the plan's count and
correlation exactly, and the sixth, Hillenmeyer against Hoepfner, differs by 66 pairs in
170{,}903 and in the third decimal. The plan's conclusion stands on the store: every pair
correlates positively once oriented, and none above 0.12.
