\section{Theoretical Limits\secstatus{todo}}
\label{sec:limits}

The question this section answers: given how much mRNA a yeast cell contains and
how little of it any method recovers, how many cells must share a perturbation
before its mean effect is measurable -- and how does that change when a cell
carries more than one perturbation?

\notesec{Two sources of noise, and only one of them is fixable by sequencing}
Every argument below turns on a distinction worth making once, plainly.
\term{Shot noise} -- also called counting or sampling noise -- is the
irreducible randomness of \emph{counting}. Sequencing a library samples molecules
at random, so a gene present at a true rate $\mu$ is observed as a count that
fluctuates Poisson-like around $\mu$, with variance equal to $\mu$ and therefore
a relative error $1/\sqrt{\mu}$. It is a property of the measurement, not of the
cell, and it is the one kind of noise that buying more reads removes.
\term{Biological overdispersion} is the rest: two genetically identical cells in
the same flask genuinely differ in how much of a gene they are expressing,
because of cell-cycle phase, metabolic state and transcriptional bursting.
Sequencing each cell to infinite depth measures each cell perfectly and leaves
this variation completely untouched; only counting \emph{more cells} averages it
down. Keeping the two apart is what \cref{sec:design-equation} is for -- the
whole design reduces to which of them is currently binding.

The disagreement is real and is left visible rather than averaged away: the
$3{,}000$ genes at $3.5$ molecules each implied by the review works out to
$\sim$10{,}500 mRNA per cell, materially below the 20{,}000 floor of the range
Jariani et al.\ cites and well below the 40{,}000--60{,}000 Jackson et al.\ give.

\notesec{This choice is not harmless, and the working value mixes two sources}
It is tempting to treat the spread as a detail, and it is not. The design
equation reaches this quantity through $p_j$, the transcriptome share of the
target gene, and $p_j$ enters only the shot-noise term -- where both candidate
platforms sit (\cref{sec:design-equation}), so $n^{*}$ scales with it almost
directly. Taking $3.5$ molecules per gene against each candidate denominator, at
$d=410$ and the measured effect size, gives

\pfstep{Read the spread, not the level} The level here is not what
\cref{sec:application} budgets against, and the two must not be confused.
\cref{eq:design} pairs an effect size with an overdispersion, because
$\varphi$ is pinned by requiring the 100-cell convention to fall out of the
equation's floor: at the measured 1.34-fold, $A=90.9$ and $\varphi=1.1$; at a
nominal two-fold, $A=16.3$ and $\varphi=6.1$. Both give a 100-cell floor, and
the second pairing gives 219, 441 and 782 for the same three denominators. So
the absolute number moves by a factor of three with the effect-size assumption,
while the \emph{spread} across denominators stays at five-fold either way. The
spread is the finding; it is a property of the unresolved $p_j$, and it is why
measuring $p_j$ in our own pilot data retires more uncertainty than any other
cheap measurement in this document.

Worse, the working value is not internally consistent. The document uses
$p_j = 3.5/30{,}000$, which pairs the review's per-gene figure with Brettner's
total -- but the $3.5$ was derived \emph{against} the review's own
$\sim$10{,}500, so used self-consistently it would give $p_j=3.3\times10^{-4}$
and cut every cells-per-perturbation figure by a factor of $2.9$. The 30{,}000
pairing is the conservative one, which is why it is kept, but it is a choice and
not a measurement, and \cref{tab:design-params} accordingly marks $p_j$ derived.

The comparison that does matter is across organisms -- a yeast cell
holds roughly four times the mRNA of an \org{E.\ coli} cell in rich medium over
$\sim$1.4$\times$ the gene count, which is why the same split-pool chemistry
recovers $\sim$410 mRNA UMIs per cell in yeast and 235--397 in bacteria.

\notesec{What would settle it, concretely} The equation does not need total mRNA
per cell. It needs $p_j$, the fraction of a cell's mRNA belonging to the target
gene, and the three-way disagreement above exists only because $p_j$ is
currently being \emph{assembled} from two sources that do not belong together: a
per-gene molecule count from one paper over a per-cell total from another.

A pilot measures $p_j$ directly and skips the assembly. Any run that yields a
count matrix gives it: take the UMIs assigned to a gene, divide by the total mRNA
UMIs in that cell, and average over cells. That is one line of analysis on data
the first experiment produces anyway (\cref{sec:host}), it needs no extra
sequencing and no extra sample, and it replaces a derived quantity with a
measured one for the genes we actually intend to target. Two caveats keep it
honest: $p_j$ is per-gene, so it should be reported for a handful of
representative targets rather than as one number, and it is condition-dependent,
so it belongs to the environment it was measured in.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{A denominator nobody has measured moves the whole budget five-fold.}
How much total mRNA a yeast cell holds is disputed by a factor of six across
three sources, and cells per perturbation moves with it. The design equation
does not actually need that total: it needs $p_j$, the target gene's share of
the transcriptome, which any count matrix yields directly as gene UMIs over
total mRNA UMIs per cell. The first pilot produces one, so this is a line of
analysis rather than an experiment, and it replaces the largest derived quantity
in the budget with a measured one.
\end{keybox}

Cells profiled span 3.6 orders of magnitude across these ten studies and depth
only 1.6, and moving from the smallest study to the largest multiplies cells by
3{,}726 while dividing depth by just 9.8. Both endpoints happen to be
Nadal-Ribelles et al., six years apart

The consequence is worth stating plainly, because the figure's diagonals invite
the opposite reading. \emph{Total} UMIs is not conserved along this cloud: it
spans 3.2 orders of magnitude, which is nearly all of the variation in cell
count. Larger studies do not redistribute a fixed amount of signal over more
cells; they collect more signal. The published record is therefore not a frontier
of equal-value points, and no method here has been shown to be a better bargain
than another by sitting further out along a diagonal.

What the diagonals do encode is a statistical fact about \emph{design}, not a
summary of what has been published. In the shot-noise limit the precision of a
perturbation's estimated mean depends on how many molecules were counted in
total, not on how they were distributed over cells, so for a fixed sequencing
spend, moving along a diagonal is free and should be decided on other grounds -- instrument access, cost structure, whether a perturbation can be read out at all.
That is the lever \cref{sec:application} pulls. It is a claim about the arithmetic
in \cref{eq:design}, and the studies plotted here neither support nor contradict
it, because none of them was designed against a common budget.

Two features carry into the rest of this document, and the second has changed
recently. Published runs span $9\times10^{5}$ to $1.3\times10^{9}$ total UMIs.
At the low end that is three orders of magnitude inside what a single modern flow
cell delivers (\cref{sec:sequencing}), so those studies were bounded by protocol
throughput and budget rather than by sequencing capacity -- which is why platform
choice, not sequencer choice, is what a small screen design turns on. That is no
longer true at the top end. Nadal-Ribelles et al.'s
$1.06$-million-cell atlas needs $1.3\times10^{9}$ UMIs, within a factor of three
of the 3.2 billion read pairs in one NovaSeq X 25B lane \emph{before} counting
duplication or the rRNA fraction, so at genome scale in yeast the sequencer has
started to bind. \Cref{sec:application} prices exactly that regime.

Second, four methods now carry a perturbation readout, and they no longer sit at
one end of anything: they run from Jackson et al.'s 38{,}225 cells to
Nadal-Ribelles et al.'s $1.06$ million, and from 325\,UMIs per cell to 2{,}809.
The largest genetic screen in this figure is a yeast one, and it used neither of
the two chemistries this document has been comparing -- it used a microwell
array (\cref{sec:guide-capture}). Split-pool versus droplet is therefore
not a throughput question at the scales anyone has run; it is a question of depth
and of cost per \emph{usable} cell, which is \cref{sec:application}.

\notesec{The one experiment that moves a point right without moving it down}
Every study here is microbial, which is the axis this figure is about, and it is
why scifi-RNA-seq is absent from it: Datlinger et al.\ ran human and mouse
material only (\cref{sec:scifi}). The reason to raise it here anyway is that it
tests the claim the diagonals invite and the cloud denies. Preindexing took one
Chromium channel from $\sim$15{,}000 loaded nuclei to 383{,}000, returning
151{,}788 transcriptomes, and measured no downward trend in UMIs per cell across
droplets holding up to 15 nuclei. Cells rose by an order of magnitude at
constant depth, which is a move to the right along no diagonal at all -- exactly
the ``bigger studies collect more signal'' behavior the published record already
shows, produced deliberately and in one experiment rather than incidentally
across a literature. It also localizes what is being traded. What the cloud's
weak downward slope reflects is not a law relating cells to depth; it is that
cheap-per-cell chemistries have so far also been shallow ones. Preindexing
separates those two properties, and \cref{sec:budget} prices the separation.

\subsection{Cells per Perturbation\secstatus{todo}}
\label{sec:cells-per-pert}

Two requirements act independently, and the larger of the two binds.

\pfstep{Constraint 1 -- a biological floor} Averaging out cell-cycle phase and
metabolic state takes cells, and no amount of sequencing depth substitutes for
them. The field standard is stated explicitly by
Brandner et al.\ \citep{brandnerPooledSinglecellCRISPRa2025}: perturbations are retained only with
at least 100 cells. Brettner et al.'s own heuristic is 500 cells per condition.
The strongest corroboration is a design choice rather than a rule: the
genome-scale yeast screen of Nadal-Ribelles et
al.\ \citep{nadal-ribellesSinglecellResolvedGenotypephenotype2025} landed at an
average of 93 and 108 cells per genotype in its two conditions (medians 77 and
87). A million-cell budget spread over 3{,}500 mutants converges on the same
hundred-cell scale that the convention asserts, arrived at independently.

\pfstep{Constraint 2 -- pseudobulk depth} Pooling $n$ cells at depth $d$ gives
$K\approx n d p_j$ UMIs of gene $j$. For counts, $\mathrm{Var}(\log K)\approx1/K$,
so resolving a log$_2$ fold change $\Delta$ at power $1-\beta$ and level $\alpha$
requires
\begin{equation}
  K \;\ge\; \left(\frac{z}{\Delta\ln 2}\right)^{2},
  \qquad z = z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta} = 2.80,
  \label{eq:power}
\end{equation}
which is $\sim$16 UMIs of that gene for a two-fold change, $\sim$69 for 1.4-fold,
$\sim$158 for 1.25-fold. This is a floor, not an estimate: it counts only
sampling noise and ignores biological variance between cells, which is exactly
why the 100-cell floor is a separate and independent requirement.

Two things follow. The 488 cells that the depth constraint demands of Brettner et al.'s
chemistry reproduces his own 500-cell heuristic from an independent derivation,
which is reassurance that the framework is calibrated. And depth buys you out of
cells only until the 100-cell floor binds -- past 10x v3 chemistry, extra depth
is wasted on this question and should be spent on more perturbations instead.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Two independent constraints, and the larger one binds.} A perturbation
needs enough cells to average out cell-cycle phase and metabolic state, and
enough total molecules to beat counting noise. Depth buys you out of cells only
until the 100-cell biological floor binds, so past roughly 10x v3 depth extra
reads are wasted on this question and belong on more perturbations instead.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{Effect Size of a Single Knockdown\secstatus{todo}}
\label{sec:effect-size}

Equation~\ref{eq:power} takes the fold change $\Delta$ as given, but $\Delta$ is
an empirical property of yeast regulatory biology, and it is the parameter the
whole design is most sensitive to. It is measured here rather than assumed, from
two datasets already in the library mirror: Kemmeren et al.'s single-deletion
expression compendium \citep{kemmerenLargeScaleGeneticPerturbations2014} and
Sameith et al.'s double-deletion compendium
\citep{sameithHighresolutionGeneExpression2015}, both built locally and analyzed
below.

\notesec{What counts as a responding gene, stated before it is used} A gene is
called \emph{responding} to a deletion when $|\log_2 \mathrm{FC}| > \log_2 1.25$
against wild type, that is, when its expression moved by more than 1.25-fold in
either direction. No significance test sits behind that, and none is available:
the compendia report a log ratio per gene per strain, so the criterion is a
magnitude cut, applied identically to all three datasets. The cut is a choice, it
is the one this document argues from, and \cref{fig:effect-size}c shows what
every alternative choice would have given.

Three findings, and the first two change the arithmetic of the whole document.

\pfstep{A deletion moves hundreds of genes, but almost none of them by two-fold}
The median single deletion moves 220--269 genes by more than 1.25$\times$, but
only 10--15 by more than 2$\times$ (\cref{tab:effect-size}). Designing a screen at
a two-fold threshold therefore targets a few percent of the transcriptional
response and discards the rest. Kemmeren's 1{,}484 single deletions and Sameith's
82 agree closely, which is reassurance that this is a property of yeast rather
than of one platform.

\pfstep{The median responding gene moves 1.34-fold, and that number inherits the
1.25 cut} Among genes clearing the 1.25$\times$ criterion, the median $|\Delta|$
is $0.42$ in log$_2$ units, i.e.\ 1.34-fold, and the three compendia agree to
within $0.02$ despite differing in size by a factor of eighteen. This is the
number Eq.~\ref{eq:power} wants.

Where it comes from is worth being exact about, because 1.34 looks like a
measured property of a yeast deletion and is not one. The $|\Delta|$ distribution
decays monotonically from zero (\cref{fig:effect-size}a), so selecting its upper
tail at any cut returns a median sitting just above that cut. Recomputed across
the ladder, the median response among responders reads 1.16$\times$ at a 1.1$\times$ cut,
1.36$\times$ at 1.25$\times$, 1.73$\times$ at 1.5$\times$ and 2.57$\times$ at
2$\times$ (\cref{fig:effect-size}c, right axis) -- close to a straight line
against the cut itself. So 1.34 is the arithmetic consequence of calling
1.25$\times$ a response, and the defensible content of this subsection is not
the value but the pair: at a 1.25$\times$ definition, a deletion moves a few
hundred genes and the typical one of them moves 1.34-fold.

That pair is what a design has to be built on, and both halves matter, because
the two move in opposite directions. A stricter cut raises $|\Delta|$, which
makes each surviving gene cheaper to resolve, and cuts the responder count, which
makes the screen see less. Because the power coefficient scales as
$|\Delta|^{-2}$, adopting the 1.25$\times$ pairing multiplies every
cells-per-perturbation figure in this document by $\sim$5.6 against the nominal
two-fold. It is the single largest correction in the analysis, it moves in the
expensive direction, and a reader who prefers a different definition of
responding can read its consequence off the same panel rather than having to
redo the work.

\pfstep{More perturbations make a cell noisier, not its effects larger} Sameith's
doubles move roughly twice as many genes as its singles at 1.25$\times$ (460
against 220) and three times as many at 2$\times$ (30 against 10), while the
median response among responders is unchanged (0.44 against 0.42). A second
deletion recruits additional genes into the response rather than amplifying the
existing ones.

One caveat on which way this is likely to be biased. Sameith et al.\ did not
sample gene pairs at random: the double-deletion panel is built from genes with
regulatory roles, chosen because they were expected to interact. Its responses
should therefore sit \emph{above} what a random pair of genes would produce, and
its singles above a random single. Kemmeren's 1{,}484 deletions are the closer
thing to an unbiased draw, and its median response, $0.42$, is essentially
identical to Sameith's $0.42$ -- so the bias does not appear to move the median
\emph{effect size} much. Where it should be assumed to matter is the responder
\emph{counts}, and the doubles in particular: 460 responding genes per double
deletion is an upper estimate for a randomly chosen pair, not a typical one. That is the favourable reading for multiplexing: the per-gene
effect a screen must resolve does not shrink as $k$ rises, so
\cref{eq:design-total} may treat $|\Delta|$ as fixed in $k$. The comparison is
made \emph{within} Sameith -- same lab, platform and noise floor -- because
comparing Kemmeren's singles against Sameith's doubles would confound perturbation
count with study.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{The design target is 1.34-fold, and it is expensive.} Every
cells-per-perturbation number derived under a two-fold assumption understates the
requirement by $\sim$5.6$\times$. What this does \emph{not} do is invalidate the
100-cell convention -- the opposite, as \cref{sec:design-equation} shows: at the
measured effect size that convention corresponds to an ordinary biological
overdispersion rather than an implausible one.
\end{keybox}

\notesec{What is still missing} These compendia are bulk microarray, so they
measure the \emph{effect size} $|\Delta|$ and say nothing about $\varphi_j$, the
between-cell variance, which needs single-cell data. Of the three unmeasured
inputs to \cref{eq:design}, this settles one.

\subsection{Multiplexing and Interaction Coverage\secstatus{todo}}
\label{sec:multiplex}

The end goal is multiplexed screens, because engineered strains carry stacked
perturbations. The arithmetic splits cleanly into a part that helps and a part
that does not.

\pfstep{Main effects get $k$ times cheaper} Under an additive model with random
$k$-subsets of $T$ genes and $N$ cells total, the cells informing gene $g$'s main
effect number $Nk/T$ -- independent of how many distinct combinations the library
contains. Every cell carrying $g$ contributes, whatever else it carries. So
$k$-plexing divides the cell requirement by $k$.

\pfstep{Named interactions scale far worse} The probability that a specific pair
$\{g,h\}$ both appear in a random $k$-subset is $k(k-1)/\left(T(T-1)\right)$.
Requiring $c$ cells on every pair among $T$ targets therefore needs
\begin{equation}
  N \;=\; c\,\binom{T}{2}\Big/\binom{k}{2},
  \label{eq:pairs}
\end{equation}
pairs needed over pairs delivered per cell. Yao et al.\ \citep{yaoScalableGeneticScreening2024}
derive the same expression and, importantly, supply the constant: $c=400$, being
``the number of cells needed to learn a single second-order interaction effect with
the same power as our conventional screens had to learn first-order effects
($\sim$100 cells/perturbations), assuming that four times as many cells are needed
to estimate a second-order interaction effect as a first-order effect.'' Our
implementation of Eq.~\ref{eq:pairs} reproduces their published worked cases to
within rounding -- 100{,}000 cells at $k=3$ powers all pairs among 39.2 targets
against their stated $\sim$39, and $10^{6}$ cells at $k=8$ gives 374.7 against
their 374 -- which is the check that the constant and the combinatorics are being
applied correctly.

\input{tables/t10-multiplex}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{The design that follows.} Genome-wide pairwise epistasis is out of reach at
any plex: $2.4\times10^{9}$ cells at $k=3$, still $2.6\times10^{8}$ at $k=8$. But a
\emph{focused} library inverts the picture, because Eq.~\ref{eq:pairs} is quadratic
in $T$ and quadratic in $k$. A 200-gene metabolic sub-library at $k=8$ needs
$\sim$284{,}000 cells for \emph{every} one of its 19{,}900 pairs -- one
genome-scale-sized run. That is the single most useful number in this document for
the multiplexed screening goal.
\end{keybox}

So the programme splits cleanly: run genome-wide at $k\approx3$ for main effects,
where compressed decoding applies and interactions are assumed to cancel; and run a
focused metabolic sub-library at high plex for named epistasis, where they are the
signal.

\pfstep{Three costs the cell arithmetic hides} Guide detection compounds:
assigning $k$ guides at per-guide detection $q$ succeeds at $q^{k}$, and the two
published rates bracket a very wide range. At Brandner et al.'s 25\% in bacteria,
even after enrichment, a 3-plex assigns 1.6\% of cells and an 8-plex is
arithmetically hopeless; at the $>$71\% Nadal-Ribelles et al.\ reach in yeast, the
same figures are 36\% and 6\%. This -- not sequencing -- is what actually caps
plex here, and which end of that range applies is the single most valuable thing
the pilot can measure (Sec.~\ref{sec:guide-capture}). Barcode collisions change
character: in a singles screen a collision attenuates a main effect slightly, but in
a multiplex screen it \emph{manufactures a gene pair that was never constructed},
and pairs are precisely what is being measured. And Yao et al.'s own screens, despite
being designed for this, recovered no significant pairwise interaction even at
$q<0.5$ -- a caution that the 400-cell constant is a power calculation, not a
demonstration.

\subsection{Is the Response Matrix Compressible?\secstatus{todo}}
\label{sec:compression}

Sec.~\ref{sec:yao} states compressed Perturb-seq's premise -- that the number of
composite samples needed scales as $(q+r)\log n$ rather than $n$, for a library
of $n$ targets, an effect matrix of rank $r$ and $q$ non-zeros per module -- and
leaves all three unplannable. Two of them are measurable from compendia already
in the mirror, because Kemmeren et al.'s 1{,}484 single-deletion profiles
\emph{are} a perturbation-by-gene effect matrix for yeast: the same object a
compressed screen would be recovering, in our organism rather than in THP1 cells.

\notesec{A warning about the letter $q$} This document now uses $q$ for two
unrelated things, and the collision is worth naming rather than leaving for a
reader to trip over. In \cref{sec:guide-capture} and \cref{sec:bottlenecks} $q$
is the \emph{per-guide detection probability}, the chance a guide present in a
cell is seen in that cell's reads. Here it is Yao et al.'s \emph{non-zeros per
module}. Both are theirs, not ours, which is why neither is renamed. An
expression compendium can measure the second and says nothing whatever about the
first, since it contains no guides.

\pfstep{The matrix is strongly low rank} Four components carry half the variance
of the whole compendium and 177 carry 90\%, against 1{,}484 rows; the
participation ratio, which needs no cut-off, is 8.4
(\cref{fig:compression}a). A deletion screen in yeast is not 6{,}000 independent
experiments, it is a few dozen recurring programs sampled 1{,}484 times, which is
the property compression exists to exploit and it is present.

\pfstep{The sparsity is real per perturbation and absent per component} One
deletion moves a median of 269 genes of 6{,}169 at the 1.25$\times$ criterion of
\cref{sec:effect-size} -- 4\% of the transcriptome, genuinely sparse. A singular
component is not sparse at all: it takes 2{,}155 genes to carry 90\% of one's
loading mass (\cref{fig:compression}b). That gap is not a defect in the
measurement, it is the reason FR-Perturb factorises by \emph{sparse} PCA rather
than by PCA, which \cref{sec:yao} reports as a design choice without saying what
it buys. Sparsity is in the rows of this matrix, not in its singular basis, and a
method has to go looking for it.

\pfstep{Compression pays only above a library size, and the panel is below it}
Evaluated at $q=269$ and $r=177$, $(q+r)\log n$ crosses $n$ near
$n\approx3{,}600$ (\cref{fig:compression}d). At genome scale it is $0.65n$; for
the 200-gene focused panel of \cref{sec:multiplex} it is $11.8n$, so on a panel
compression costs an order of magnitude \emph{more} samples than measuring each
target directly. The constant is unknown and shifts the crossing, but not the
existence of one: the compressed requirement is nearly flat in $n$ and the
conventional one is linear, so they must cross, and the only question is where.
Plain guide-pooling, which divides samples by $m$ and assumes nothing about
sparsity, sits below both across the whole range this document contemplates
(\cref{fig:compression}e).

\pfstep{And the assumption underneath it does not hold in the additive direction}
Guide-pooling requires that effects combine additively in log expression space,
and Yao et al.\ do not claim interactions are absent -- they claim interactions
\emph{cancel}, because each cell draws a random combination and positive and
negative average out. That is a claim about a mean, and Sameith et al.'s doubles
test it directly. Over the 72 doubles whose two singles were profiled by the same
lab on the same platform, the observed response correlates well with the additive
prediction (median Pearson $r=0.87$) but is systematically \emph{smaller}: the
median slope of observed on predicted is 0.62 (\cref{fig:compression}c). Two
deletions produce about 60\% of the sum of their singles. Interactions in yeast
do not cancel here; they buffer.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Compression is a poor fit for the screen this document is designing, and
the data says so in three independent ways.} The library size that makes it pay
($n\gtrsim3{,}600$) is above the focused panel where interactions are actually
reachable; plain guide-pooling beats it at every $n$ considered; and the
additivity its decoding assumes is violated in yeast in a systematic direction,
with doubles at 0.62 of additive rather than scattering around 1. What the
measurement \emph{does} support is the simpler half of the same idea: the matrix
is low rank, so pooling and averaging recovers main effects efficiently, which is
what \cref{fig:economics}f already prices.
\end{keybox}

\notesec{Three reasons not to over-read the 0.62} Sameith et al.\ did not sample
gene pairs at random -- the panel is built from regulatory genes chosen because
they were expected to interact, so its deviation from additivity should sit
\emph{above} what a random pair would show, and 0.62 is therefore a lower bound
on additivity rather than an estimate of it. Seventy-two pairs is a small sample.
And $m=2$ is the only multiplicity anyone has measured: whether attenuation
compounds, saturates or reverses at $m=10$ is exactly the question
\cref{sec:effect-size} could not answer for responder counts either. \textbf{
Hypothesis (untested):} buffering at $m=2$ predicts a shortfall at higher $m$,
which would make pooled main effects weaker than the $1/m$ scaling of
\cref{fig:economics}f assumes. The two-guide pilot of \cref{sec:staging} is what
would settle it, and this is a second reason to run it.

\subsection{Library Construction Limits\secstatus{todo}}
\label{sec:library-ceiling}

Multiplexing is limited by cloning and transformation before it is limited by
sequencing, and the binding number comes from our own library's paper.

%% SOURCE: experiments/019-perturb-seq-costing/scripts/method_data.py
%%   YEAST_TRANSFORMATION_CLONES_{LOW,HIGH}, LIBRARY_REDUNDANCY_FOLD,
%%   clones_required(); pair counts from cost_model.py.
Lian et al.\ \citep{lianMultifunctionalGenomewideCRISPR2019} put yeast
transformation at $10^{6}$--$10^{7}$ independent clones and set their own
construction standard at $>10^{6}$ clones with \emph{at least 30-fold
redundancy}. Those two numbers decide the question. A dual-guide library over
6{,}000 genes has $\binom{6000}{2}=1.8\times10^{7}$ pairs, which at 30-fold
redundancy needs $5.4\times10^{8}$ independent clones -- 54 to 540 times beyond
the ceiling. Genome-scale pairwise coverage is not merely expensive to construct;
it is unreachable by about two orders of magnitude, and no sequencing budget
changes that.

\notesec{The precedent already ran this experiment} sMAGIC is the combinatorial
version of our own library, and it hit exactly this wall: ``due to the
limitations in transformation efficiency ($\sim10^{6}$--$10^{7}$ for yeast
transformation), only $\sim$0.01\% to $\sim$0.1\% of the total combinations
($\sim10^{5}\times10^{5}=10^{10}$) were covered.'' That is the same arithmetic
seen from the other side, and it carries a design lesson: they reached those
combinations by \emph{co-transforming} single-guide libraries rather than by
cloning pairs. Co-transformation converts a construction problem into a Poisson
sampling problem -- diversity becomes free, but which pairs you get is random,
so coverage of a \emph{named} pair is governed by \cref{eq:pairs} rather than by
the library design. Assembly is not the obstacle either way: Golden-Gate
efficiency is ``nearly 100\%''.

\notesec{Restricting the panel is what makes it constructible} The same
arithmetic that rules out the genome-scale library admits the focused one.
A 200-gene metabolic sub-library has $\binom{200}{2}=19{,}900$ pairs, needing
597{,}000 clones at 30-fold redundancy -- comfortably inside a single
transformation. This is the same conclusion \cref{sec:multiplex} reaches from
cell counts, arrived at independently from cloning: panel size, not plex, is the
variable that decides whether named epistasis is reachable, and it binds in both
the wet lab and the budget.

\Cref{fig:ceiling} sweeps both variables rather than evaluating the two points
above, and the crossing is sharper than the genome-scale conclusion suggests.
Cloning pairs caps the panel at 258 genes at $10^{6}$ clones and 816 at
$10^{7}$; cloning triples caps it at 59 and 126. So the reachable panel is not
somewhat smaller than the genome, it is two orders of magnitude smaller, and it
shrinks by roughly another order for each additional guide cloned onto the
plasmid.

\pfstep{Which is the argument for co-transformation, stated as a picture}
Panel (b) is the one worth looking at twice. Co-transforming single-guide
libraries needs $30T$ clones \emph{whatever $k$ is}, because the clone count
scales with the number of guides rather than with the number of combinations.
That is 180{,}000 clones to cover all 6{,}000 genes, inside one transformation,
at any plex. The combinations then come from Poisson uptake
(\cref{sec:delivery}) instead of from cloning. Nothing about this makes a
\emph{named} pair cheaper to observe -- \cref{eq:pairs} still governs that, and
\cref{sec:recovery} shows what it costs -- but it removes construction from the
list of reasons multiplexing is hard, and leaves only the measurement.

\subsection{A Unified Design Equation\secstatus{todo}}
\label{sec:design-equation}

The preceding subsections give three rules that look independent: a shot-noise
requirement (\cref{eq:power}), a 100-cell biological floor, and a combinatorial
penalty for multiplexing. Sections~\ref{sec:cells-per-pert} and \ref{sec:multiplex}
combine the first two by taking whichever is larger. They are not independent, and
the max is an artefact of having modeled the count as Poisson.

%% DONE(zotero, 2026-08-21) -- all eight works are now in the microbe-perturb-seq
%% collection and carry numbered citations, so tables/t13-external-refs.tex and
%% scripts/external_refs.py are retired. The standing debt this paragraph used to
%% record is paid; do not reintroduce a DOI table for works we hold.
This is not a new result. It is the standard negative-binomial sample-size
calculation from the bulk RNA-seq literature, applied to a pseudobulk pool.
\Cref{eq:design} appears in essentially this form in Hart et
al.\ \citep{hartCalculatingSampleSize2013}: a sum of a sampling term and a
dispersion term over the squared log fold change. The parameter $\varphi_j$ is
the negative-binomial dispersion of McCarthy et
al.\ \citep{mccarthyDifferentialExpressionAnalysis2012}, whose biological
coefficient of variation is $\sqrt{\varphi_j}$ -- so published BCV values
transfer directly as priors for the one input we cannot measure -- and the
variance parameterization itself is that of Robinson and
Smyth \citep{robinsonSmallsampleEstimationNegative2008}. The decomposition into
Poisson sampling on top of biological variation is the single-cell UMI noise model
of Gr\"un et al.\ \citep{grunValidationNoiseModels2014}, and using a plain
negative binomial rather than a zero-inflated one follows
Svensson \citep{svenssonDropletScRNAseqNot2020}. Treating the estimand as a
pseudobulk profile rather than a per-cell test follows Squair et
al.\ \citep{squairConfrontingFalseDiscoveries2021}; the cells-versus-depth optimum
that \cref{fig:design}a traces under a fixed budget is the subject of Zhang et
al.\ \citep{zhangDeterminingSequencingDepth2020}; and the largest published
Perturb-seq screen, whose empirical cells-per-perturbation choice
\cref{sec:cells-per-pert} is measured against, is Replogle et
al.\ \citep{replogleMappingInformationrichGenotypephenotype2022}. What is specific
to this document is the multiplexing term of \cref{eq:design-total} and the
reading of the 100-cell convention below.

\pfstep{Setup} Pool $n$ cells carrying one perturbation, each read to depth $d$
mRNA UMIs, and consider gene $j$ with transcriptome share $p_j$. The pseudobulk
count is $K=n d p_j$. Treating it as negative binomial, with $\varphi_j$ the
gene's biological overdispersion between cells -- the variance in excess of
sampling -- \begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Var}(\log K)\;\approx\;\underbrace{\frac{1}{n d p_j}}_{\text{shot noise}}
  \;+\;\underbrace{\frac{\varphi_j}{n}}_{\text{biology}} .
  \label{eq:nbvar}
\end{equation}

\pfstep{Claim} Requiring a log$_2$ fold change $\Delta$ to be resolvable against
controls at level $\alpha$ and power $1-\beta$ gives one requirement,
\begin{equation}
  n^{*} \;=\; A(\Delta)\left[\frac{1}{d\,p_j}+\varphi_j\right],
  \qquad
  A(\Delta)=\frac{\kappa\,(z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta})^{2}}{(\Delta\ln 2)^{2}},
  \label{eq:design}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa=1$ when the non-targeting control pool is large enough that its own
variance is negligible -- the usual case, since controls are pooled over many
guides -- and $\kappa=2$ for a control of the same size as the perturbation.
and with $k$ guides per cell over a panel of $T$ targets the total cells for an
order-$r$ effect are
\begin{equation}
  N^{*} \;=\; \rho_r\, n^{*}\,\frac{\binom{T}{r}}{\binom{k}{r}}
  \cdot\frac{1}{s\,q^{r}} ,
  \label{eq:design-total}
\end{equation}
with $\rho_r$ the contrast variance inflation, $s$ the QC survival fraction and
$q$ the per-guide detection probability. $\rho_1{=}1$ by definition. $\rho_2{=}4$
is \emph{quoted} from Yao et al.\ rather than derived: it is the variance of a
full $2\times2$ factorial contrast over four equally sized groups. Under the same
large-control assumption used for main effects the exact factor is 3, so Yao's 4
is conservative by about a third; it is kept because the 400-cells-per-pair
constant of \cref{sec:multiplex} is built on it and the two must not disagree.

\pfstep{Consequence} \Cref{eq:design} contains both earlier rules as limits.
Setting $\varphi_j\to0$ leaves $n^{*}=A/(d\,p_j)$, that is a required pseudobulk
count $K=n^{*}d\,p_j=A$; at $\kappa=1$ this is \emph{identically}
\cref{eq:power}, so \cref{sec:cells-per-pert} is the shot-noise corner of this
equation rather than a separate rule. Letting $d\,p_j\to\infty$ gives
$n^{*}\to A\varphi_j$, a floor no amount of sequencing can cross -- which
\emph{is} the biological floor, now with a number attached. At
80\% power $A=16.3$ at two-fold and $A=90.9$ at the measured 1.34-fold, so a
100-cell floor is precisely the claim $\varphi_j\approx6.1$ or
$\varphi_j\approx1.1$ respectively.

That number depends on $\Delta$, and this is where measuring the effect size pays
off. At the nominal two-fold the 100-cell floor implies $\varphi_j\approx6.1$, a
between-cell coefficient of variation of $2.5$ -- bursty even by single-cell
standards, and grounds for suspecting the convention was really guarding against
something other than sampling variance. At the \emph{measured} 1.34-fold
(\cref{sec:effect-size}) the same floor implies $\varphi_j\approx1.1$, a
coefficient of variation of about one, which is an entirely ordinary
single-cell overdispersion. The tension dissolves: the 100-cell convention is
consistent with unremarkable yeast biology once it is evaluated at the effect
size screens are actually built to detect. The convention was right; the two-fold
assumption was wrong.

\pfstep{Interpretation -- what would make this predictive} As a structure
\crefrange{eq:nbvar}{eq:design-total} are complete, and every design question in
\cref{sec:application} is a substitution into them. As a \emph{predictor} they are
not usable yet, because two of their inputs are still assumed rather than
measured. Three were, and the list below is kept at three so the one that has
since been pinned can be read against the two that have not:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{$\varphi_j$, gene-level biological overdispersion in yeast.} The
    critical one: it alone sets the floor, and the whole 100-cell convention rests
    on it. Estimable from any existing yeast scRNA-seq count matrix, and it is
    the reason the figure sweeps $\varphi$ instead of fixing it.
  \item \textbf{The distribution of $\Delta$ -- now measured.} Median
    $|\Delta|=0.42$ among responding genes, a $1.34\times$ change
    (\cref{sec:effect-size}, \cref{tab:effect-size}). $A(\Delta)$ goes as
    $\Delta^{-2}$, so this was the most violent term in the equation; pinning it
    multiplied every cell requirement by $\sim$5.6 and resolved the $\varphi$
    puzzle above. \Cref{tab:design-params} now carries it as measured, and
    $A(\Delta)$ with it as derived rather than assumed.
  \item \textbf{$q$, per-guide detection probability.} Enters as $q^{r}$, so it
    is forgiving for main effects and punishing for interactions. It cannot be
    measured until the guide-barcode construct of \cref{sec:guide-capture}
    exists.
\end{enumerate}

Of the three, one is now measured and two remain. The curves here therefore rest
on one measured parameter and one swept one, which is why $\varphi$ is drawn as a
family rather than a line, and why no number from this subsection is yet used in
the budgets of \cref{sec:application}. Those budgets are built on the 100, 250 and
500 cells-per-gene tiers, which come from the biological floor rather than from a
fold-change target, so they are NOT understated by the 5.6$\times$ that
\cref{sec:effect-size} applies to a depth-derived requirement. The
5.6$\times$ bites only where a number was derived from the two-fold assumption,
and \cref{sec:design-equation} shows the convention itself survives the measured
effect size intact.

Two limitations of the derivation itself, for completeness. The delta-method step
behind \cref{eq:nbvar} degrades when counts are small, and $A\approx16$ counts is
small -- near the shot-noise limit \cref{eq:design} should be read as an
order-of-magnitude statement, with an exact negative-binomial test the right tool
in that corner. And \cref{eq:design} assumes equal per-cell depth; real libraries
vary and are handled with size factors, so $d$ should be read as a mean depth,
correct to first order. Neither affects the structural conclusions, both of which
concern the $\varphi$ term. The published treatment of the cells-versus-depth
optimum that panel~(a) rediscovers is Zhang et
al.\ \citep{zhangDeterminingSequencingDepth2020}, and the case for pseudobulk as
the estimand is Squair et al.\ \citep{squairConfrontingFalseDiscoveries2021}.

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{One equation contains both design rules, and they were never
independent.} Setting biological overdispersion to zero recovers the shot-noise
requirement exactly; letting depth run to infinity recovers the biological
floor. The apparent tension in the 100-cell convention dissolves once the effect
size is measured rather than assumed: at the measured 1.34-fold it corresponds
to an entirely ordinary yeast overdispersion, not an implausibly bursty one. The
convention was right and the two-fold assumption was wrong.
\end{keybox}
