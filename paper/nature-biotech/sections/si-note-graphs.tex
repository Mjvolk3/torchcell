\suppnote{note:graphs}{gene--gene graphs used as attention priors\secstatus{todo}}

Nine gene--gene graphs regularize the cell encoder's attention in the trigenic experiment,
one head per graph, and every one of them enters the model only through the soft prior
$\Omega_k$ of Eq.~\ref{eq:graphreg}: none is used for message passing and none is imposed as a
hard mask. \supptab{tab:databases} and \suppfig{fig:graph-priors} report how large the graphs
are, how much they overlap, and how their neighborhoods are structured;
\suppfig{fig:graph-priors-2} which genes they make hubs and how far the two
transcription-factor graphs agree; \supptab{tab:string-versions} and
\suppfig{fig:graph-priors-2}d how the STRING channels have changed across releases.

\notesec{Sources} Two graphs are curated from the \emph{Saccharomyces} Genome Database
(SGD). \emph{Physical} holds the protein--protein interactions SGD records per gene.
\emph{Regulatory} holds SGD's regulation annotations as directed regulator$\to$target edges;
of its 39,636 records, 38,677 are transcriptional and 37,890 come from high-throughput
assays (24,199 from chromatin immunoprecipitation), so 511 regulator genes reach 6,546 target
genes and the graph touches 6,582 of the 6,607 genes in the reference. \emph{TFLink} is the
transcription-factor--target compendium of the same name (v1.0), also directed. The remaining
six are the evidence channels STRING v12.0 scores separately for \emph{S.\ cerevisiae}: gene
neighborhood, gene fusion, phylogenetic co-occurrence, co-expression, experimental
(protein-interaction assays), and curated database. A channel edge is any gene pair with a
nonzero channel score; no score threshold is applied, matching the cut DANGO trains on. Every
graph is restricted to the S288C reference gene set of 6,607 genes, and the directed graphs
are treated as undirected gene pairs wherever graphs are compared below, except where the
two transcription-factor graphs are compared with each other.

\notesec{Size and coverage} The graphs differ by a factor of three in the genes they reach
(2,191 for neighborhood, 6,474 for co-expression) and by two orders of magnitude in edges
(11,085 for co-occurrence, 996,199 for co-expression), with mean degree from 7.7 (fusion) to
308 (co-expression) (\suppfig{fig:graph-priors}a; \supptab{tab:databases}). Their degree
distributions differ in shape as well as scale (\suppfig{fig:graph-priors}b): fusion and
co-occurrence have no gene above degree 100, the two dense STRING channels fall off sharply
past degree $10^{3}$, and TFLink carries the heaviest tail, with transcription factors
reaching several thousand targets. Summed over the nine graphs the edges name 2,437,196 gene
pairs, of which 1,515,940 are distinct, 6.9\% of the 21.8 million pairs the vocabulary
allows.

\notesec{Overlap} The graphs are complementary rather than redundant. The Jaccard index of
edge sets exceeds 0.12 for a single pair, co-expression against experimental (0.44)
(\suppfig{fig:graph-priors}c). The index hides the absolute counts: those two channels share
556,106 gene pairs, physical and experimental share 104,771, and neighborhood and
co-expression 119,758, whereas the two transcription-factor graphs, regulatory and TFLink,
share 16,885 (\suppfig{fig:graph-priors-2}a). Containment is strongly asymmetric
(\suppfig{fig:graph-priors}d): 76\% of physical edges lie inside the experimental channel and
51\% inside co-expression, 82\% of neighborhood edges lie inside co-expression, and 43\% of
regulatory edges lie inside TFLink, whereas only 8\% of TFLink edges are regulatory. The two
dense channels therefore absorb much of the sparse ones without the converse holding. Counting
support per pair (\suppfig{fig:graph-priors}e), 52\% of the distinct pairs appear in exactly
one graph, 37\% in two, and none in more than seven. TFLink (80\%) and regulatory (51\%)
contribute the largest shares of edges found nowhere else; database (8\%) and neighborhood
(10\%) the smallest (\supptab{tab:databases}, Unique). How much each graph contributes to
held-out accuracy is not measured here.

\notesec{Structure} The prior of Eq.~\ref{eq:graphreg} pulls the attention row of gene $i$
toward the uniform distribution over the neighbors of $i$ in $A^{(k)}$, so what a graph
exposes to the model is its neighborhoods (\suppfig{fig:graph-priors}f). Eight graphs are
essentially one connected component, with 96\% to 100\% of their covered genes in the largest
component; co-occurrence is not, splitting into 486 components with 43\% of its genes in the
largest. The mean clustering coefficient, the fraction of a gene's neighbor pairs that are
themselves connected averaged over genes, ranges from 0.03 (fusion) to 0.67 (TFLink). Degree
assortativity, the correlation between the degrees at the two ends of an edge, is negative for
the three curated graphs (TFLink $-0.44$, regulatory $-0.37$, physical $-0.20$; hubs connect
to low-degree genes) and positive for every STRING channel except experimental ($-0.01$),
most strongly for co-occurrence (0.93), whose components are near-cliques of genes sharing a
phylogenetic profile. Two-hop reach, the number of genes within two edges of a gene averaged
over covered genes, separates the graphs by three orders of magnitude: 5,038 (TFLink), 4,895
(experimental), 4,686 (physical), 4,682 (co-expression), 3,365 (regulatory), 1,646
(neighborhood), 404 (database), 78 (fusion), and 22 (co-occurrence). The four dense graphs
place over 70\% of the vocabulary within two edges of a typical gene; fusion and
co-occurrence place under 2\%.

\notesec{Hubs} The highest-degree gene differs in every graph (\suppfig{fig:graph-priors-2}b):
DHH1 in physical (3,606 partners), FKH1 in regulatory (2,991), GCN4 in TFLink (4,908), DUR12
in neighborhood (936), CDC28 in fusion (84), SPS1 in co-occurrence (97), SSA1 in
co-expression (1,770), NAB2 in experimental (2,696), and HFD1 in database (374). The nine
top-ten lists name 74 genes, of which 12 appear in two lists and two, GDE1 and ISW1, in
three. At the top 1\% of the vocabulary (66 genes per graph), 353 genes are hubs of exactly
one graph, 79 of two, 22 of three, 3 of four, and one, CDC28, of five. A gene that dominates
one graph is usually unremarkable in the others: GCN4 tops TFLink and is absent from three
STRING channels, and the four Hsp70 chaperones SSA1, SSA2, SSB1, and SSB2 rank in the top ten
of co-expression and of one other graph each.

\notesec{Transcription-factor graphs} Regulatory and TFLink both list directed
regulator$\to$target edges, yet they agree on little beyond the targets
(\suppfig{fig:graph-priors-2}c). Regulatory names 511 regulators and TFLink 317, with 200 in
both. Targets overlap almost completely (4,963 of a 6,657-gene union) because both graphs
reach most of the genome, but of the 223,620 distinct directed edges only 16,817 (7.5\%) are
in both; 22,819 are regulatory-only and 183,984 TFLink-only. Even for the 200 shared
regulators the target sets rarely agree: the median Jaccard index between a regulator's
regulatory targets and its TFLink targets is 0.03, and the best case is FKH1 at 0.53 (1,747
shared targets). The two graphs are complementary descriptions of the same regulators rather
than two samples of one network, and they are kept as separate priors.

\notesec{STRING releases} DANGO was built on STRING v9.1 and set its per-channel weights from
the change to v11.0; the CGT experiment uses v12.0. Read in release order, every channel
grows at each step and loses part of what it had (\suppfig{fig:graph-priors-2}d;
\supptab{tab:string-versions}). From v9.1 to v11.0 a channel retains 42\% (fusion) to 72\%
(neighborhood) of its pairs and adds between 2,775 (co-occurrence) and 370,984
(co-expression) new ones; from v11.0 to v12.0 it retains 51\% (fusion) to 75\% (experimental)
and, in four of the six channels, adds more pairs than it held before. Of the pairs in a
v12.0 channel, only 17\% (fusion) to 45\% (neighborhood) were already in v11.0 and 4\%
(fusion) to 21\% (co-expression) were in v9.1; over the three releases the channels grow
2.2$\times$ (database) to 8.7$\times$ (fusion) in edges. A model trained on one release
therefore sees a substantially different prior from one trained on the next; whether that
difference moves DANGO is tested in \suppnoteref{note:dango-repro}.
